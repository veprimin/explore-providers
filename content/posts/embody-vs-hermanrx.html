Key differences at a glance: Embody vs HermanRx

This comparison focuses on the most distinctive differences supplied by the sources: monthly price, what is included in the monthly fee, shipping and review timelines, geographic availability and product type. Both providers offer compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide options; regulatory notes state compounded medications are not FDA-approved.

1. Price and what you pay monthly

Embody: Starting price is $69/month for compounded semaglutide (noted as cut from $299) and $119/month for compounded tirzepatide. HermanRx: Semaglutide + B12 is $249/month and tirzepatide + B12 is $349/month, both described as all-inclusive. The sources list Embody as the lower entry price and HermanRx as higher-priced but bundled.

2. Inclusions: B12, review, and support

HermanRx explicitly bundles vitamin B12 with its compounded GLP-1 offerings and states the monthly price covers the provider review, prescription, medication, ongoing support and shipping with no separate membership or consultation fee. Embody describes prescription and telemed visits included and 24/7 support access for dosing and side-effect questions but does not list a B12 bundle in the supplied evidence and positions itself as a no-frills, prescription-forward option.

3. Shipping and turnaround timelines

Embody promises free shipping and delivery in 1–2 days after clinician approval. HermanRx publishes a roughly 24 hour provider review and roughly 72 hour delivery for prescribed orders, and also notes a wider 3–7 business day window in its FAQ depending on pharmacy processing and location. If the fastest post-approval delivery is the priority, Embody advertises the quicker 1–2 day timeline.

4. Geographic availability and platform scope

HermanRx states availability in all 50 states and lists five treatment categories it supports, useful for consolidating multiple types of care on one platform. Embody’s evidence emphasizes fast delivery and low price; the supplied source does not list a full 50-state claim in the captured evidence. Both sources reiterate that compounded medications are not FDA-approved finished products.
