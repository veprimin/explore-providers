How they differ at a glance

Both providers require a licensed clinician to determine medical appropriateness. The most distinctive supported differences are: medication breadth (name-brand plus compounded at Shed versus injectable and oral options at BreezeMeds), coaching integration, clear promotional pricing, compounded medication regulatory notes, and shipping or discretion claims.

Medication options: name-brand and compounded vs injectable and oral

Shed offers both FDA-approved name-brand GLP-1 medications and compounded GLP-1 options on one platform, including compounded semaglutide and compounded tirzepatide. The source explicitly notes that compounded medications are not FDA-approved and are prepared by a licensed pharmacy based on a provider’s prescription. BreezeMeds lists semaglutide and tirzepatide injections and also displays oral semaglutide and oral tirzepatide from $399. BreezeMeds captures both injectable and oral formats in its landing evidence.

What that means

Shed gives a single platform choice between branded and compounded formulations, while BreezeMeds emphasizes availability of both injectable and oral formulations and lists a range of starting prices including $199 and $399 figures.

Coaching and support versus discreet delivery

Shed integrates continuous coaching into treatment so coaching is bundled into the monthly cost rather than offered as an optional add-on, and the platform states coaching supports titration, lifestyle changes, and plateaus. BreezeMeds highlights discreet home delivery and ongoing support and frames its offering around discreet shipping and personalized plans.

Pricing and promotions with preserved qualifiers

BreezeMeds shows starting prices including semaglutide injection from $199/mo, tirzepatide injection from $299, and oral options displayed from $399. A separate $169 figure appears on BreezeMeds without a clear product association.

Regulatory and clinician review notes

Both providers make clear treatment is available only when a licensed clinician determines it is medically appropriate. The Shed source explicitly states that compounded medications are not FDA-approved and are not reviewed by FDA for safety, effectiveness or quality. BreezeMeds’ evidence says medication-specific approval status must be confirmed and that branded and compounded products are not interchangeable.
