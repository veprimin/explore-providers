Regenics vs Refills: both are telehealth platforms offering compounded GLP-1 medications from U.S. pharmacies, but they differ in price structure, clinician workflows, and added services. Early verdict: Refills is cheaper at entry price while Regenics highlights nurse-practitioner oversight and a Sermorelin add-on.

Explore Regenics pricing and plan details

How pricing and promotional terms compare

Price is the clearest difference in the Regenics vs Refills comparison. Refills advertises compounded semaglutide from $99/mo and tirzepatide at $159/mo with flat pricing the same at every dosage and a noted new-patient promotion. Regenics markets compounded semaglutide at $139 first month and tirzepatide at $189 first month, with stated renewals at higher monthly rates: semaglutide renewing at $199/month and tirzepatide at $279/month according to Regenics materials. Regenics labels its lower amounts as first-month promotional pricing.

Clinician review, care model, and ongoing support

Both services require a licensed clinician to review intake before prescribing. Refills describes a health intake quiz that leads to clinician review, possible chat with a doctor, and clinician consultations at no extra cost for questions about progress, side effects, or dosage. Refills also promotes 24/7 health concierge support and month-to-month plans with cancel-anytime terms. Regenics emphasizes nurse-practitioner-led GLP-1 care; licensed nurse practitioners review intake before anything is prescribed and oversee every prescription.

Medication sourcing, regulation, and transparency

Both companies offer compounded medications prepared by U.S. pharmacies and explicitly note that compounded products are not FDA-approved finished products and are not reviewed by the FDA for safety, effectiveness, or quality. Regenics states its pharmacy network is LegitScript certified and that medications are compounded by licensed U.S. pharmacies. Refills also notes use of U.S. compounding pharmacies, third-party USP testing, and LegitScript certification in its materials. Both require prescription and clinician determination that treatment is medically appropriate.

Shipping, membership, and convenience features

Refills advertises free discreet and express shipping, no membership or app fees, and a monthly refill workflow with tracking updates and a 4-week supply mailed each month. Refills says it is available in all 50 states and describes the month-to-month structure with no contracts or cancellation fees. Regenics advertises free shipping with medication compounded in the USA but captured content did not verify details about membership, cancellation fees, or state availability beyond the shipping claim on the captured page.

Distinctive extra offerings

Regenics highlights Sermorelin peptide therapy as an available add-on, a 15mg injectable peptide therapy priced at $399 in its listed FAQ material. Refills highlights availability of a GIP+GLP-1 dual agonist option and a 24/7 health concierge. These features differentiate service scope: Regenics adds a secondary peptide line, while Refills emphasizes flat pricing across dosages and ongoing concierge access.

Safety, limits, and clinician determination

Both sources clearly state that compounded medications are not FDA-approved finished products and that a licensed clinician must determine whether treatment is medically appropriate. Regenics repeatedly frames care as nurse-practitioner-led and subject to clinician review. Refills describes clinician review after intake quiz and notes prescriptions are provided if deemed appropriate by partner clinicians.

Bottom line and how to choose

Regenics vs Refills comes down to priorities: Refills offers lower advertised entry pricing and flat pricing at every dosage plus concierge support and explicit month-to-month terms. Regenics emphasizes nurse-practitioner-led oversight, a LegitScript-certified compounding network, and a Sermorelin peptide add-on with promotional first-month pricing that renews at higher monthly rates. Both use U.S. compounding pharmacies and note that compounded products are not FDA-approved.
