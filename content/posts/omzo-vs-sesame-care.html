Omzo vs Sesame Care: three key differences to consider

This comparison uses only the supplied evidence to compare Omzo and Sesame Care directly across the most distinctive differences: medication model and regulatory notes, pricing structure, and clinician access and care model.

1) Medication model and regulatory status

Omzo: The platform connects you with licensed U.S. providers who may prescribe compounded medications prepared by state-licensed sterile compounding pharmacies. The source explicitly states compounded drugs are not FDA-approved and have not been reviewed by the FDA for safety, effectiveness, or quality. Providers may alternatively write prescriptions for branded, FDA-approved products, but Omzo does not sell, dispense, or ship branded medications.

Sesame Care: The marketplace offers access to oral and injectable branded GLP-1 medications, including FDA-approved options such as the Wegovy pill and Zepbound KwikPen when appropriate. The source notes medication-specific approval status must be confirmed and that branded and compounded products are not interchangeable. Sesame Care separates subscription care from medication costs and notes medication costs vary by insurance and pharmacy.

2) Pricing structure and what is included

Omzo: Pricing evidence shows semaglutide starting at $179 and tirzepatide starting at $279. The Omzo description repeatedly lists those starting prices and indicates medication costs are included in fees collected by Omzo and paid to partner pharmacies unless otherwise noted. The source emphasizes confirming eligibility, total cost, renewal terms and state availability before enrollment.

Sesame Care: The marketplace advertises ongoing video visits, labs and messaging for as low as $59 per month based on a 12-month Success by Sesame commitment. Cash-pay GLP-1 medications are shown as low as $149 per month. The source also highlights that medication costs are not included in the subscription price and will vary based on insurance status and pharmacy, with insurance accepted for weight loss medications.

3) Clinician access and care model

Omzo: Clinical services are provided by third party networks of U.S.-licensed clinicians who determine eligibility and make final prescribing decisions. Omzo states it is not a medical practice and that the online assessment does not create a doctor patient relationship. The model is described as clinician-guided plans reviewed by licensed U.S. providers.

Sesame Care: Described as a telehealth marketplace, Sesame lets patients choose their own clinician from a large network, browse by specialty, availability and pricing, and book flexible appointments. The source positions Sesame as giving patients more control over the care relationship, with ongoing video visits, dedicated provider care, labs and messaging available under subscription.
